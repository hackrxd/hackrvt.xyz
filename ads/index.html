the 2,000 year old computer

in the spring of 1900, a group of greek sponge divers sheltering from a storm dropped anchor near antikythera, a tiny island between crete and the mainland. one of them dove to check the seabed and surfaced in a panic, babbling about a pile of dead naked women. what he had actually found was a roman-era shipwreck, its cargo of bronze and marble statues scattered across the bottom. the greek government eventually organised a salvage operation, and over the following year the divers pulled up sculptures, pottery, jewellery and coins, along with a lot of corroded junk that nobody thought much about.

one of those lumps of junk sat in the national archaeological museum in athens for months before it fell apart. in may 1902 the archaeologist valerios stais noticed that the crust had cracked open and revealed a gear wheel embedded in the rock. at first people assumed it was some kind of astronomical clock, or maybe an astrolabe, but that seemed impossible. nothing like it was supposed to exist in the ancient world. for decades the device was treated as an oddity, and a few scholars even suggested it had to have been dropped there by a later ship.

the real picture only started to emerge in the 1950s, when the historian derek de solla price began studying it seriously, and then in the 1970s when radiography revealed the hidden internal structure. later, in the 2000s, researchers using three dimensional x-ray computed tomography were able to read tiny inscriptions on the fragments, effectively an ancient user manual, and to count the teeth of each gear. the machine, now known as the antikythera mechanism, dates to somewhere between the 2nd and 1st centuries BC. it was housed in a wooden case about the size of a shoebox, and contained at least 30 meshing bronze gears, each cut by hand with triangular teeth.

what it did is remarkable. when the user turned a crank on the side, pointers on the front dial moved to show the positions of the sun and moon against the zodiac, along with the phase of the moon. the back of the device had two large spiral dials: one tracked the 19 year metonic cycle, which is how the greeks reconciled lunar months with the solar year, and the other tracked the saros cycle, an 18 year pattern that can be used to predict eclipses. a smaller dial counted out the four year cycle of the panhellenic games, including the olympics.

the cleverest part is how it handled the moon. the moon doesn't move across the sky at a constant speed, because its orbit is an ellipse, so it speeds up and slows down over each month. greek astronomers knew this, and the mechanism accounts for it with a pair of gears whose axes are slightly offset from each other, one of which carries a pin that rides in a slot on the other. the result is a gear train whose output speed wobbles in just the right way to imitate the moon's real motion. it is a mechanical model of a mathematical theory, built a couple of thousand years before anyone thought to call that a computer.

what makes the find even stranger is the silence around it. as far as we can tell, nothing of comparable mechanical sophistication appears again for roughly a thousand years, until the astronomical clocks of medieval europe. we don't know who built it, although names such as archimedes, hipparchus and the stoic philosopher posidonius of rhodes have all been suggested. it was almost certainly not a one-off, since a machine this refined doesn't come from nowhere, but if there were others, they were melted down or lost to time. we only have this one because a ship happened to sink at the right spot.

research is still going. in 2021 a team at university college london published a proposed reconstruction of the entire front of the device, explaining how a system of concentric tubes could drive the planets as well as the sun and moon, and it was based entirely on the surviving fragments and the text inscribed on them. more than a century after it came out of the water, the mechanism is still giving up its secrets.
